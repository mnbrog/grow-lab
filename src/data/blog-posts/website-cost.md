Ask three agencies what a website costs and you'll get three wildly different numbers. One quotes $500, another quotes $15,000, and a third sends a proposal so full of line items you need a spreadsheet to read it. None of that helps you budget.

So here's a straight answer, along with what actually drives the price.

## The Short Answer

For a small business in 2026, you're realistically looking at one of three routes:

1. **DIY builder** — $20 to $50 a month, plus 20 to 40 hours of your own time
2. **Freelancer or agency build** — $3,000 to $12,000 upfront, then ongoing maintenance
3. **Managed monthly plan** — $150 to $500 a month, with everything folded in

The gap between those isn't really about quality. It's about who is doing the work, and who keeps doing it after launch day.

## What You're Actually Paying For

A website is not a one-time purchase. It's a small piece of software that has to stay online, stay fast, and stay current. That means the real cost breaks into:

- **Design and build** — the part everyone quotes
- **Hosting** — where the site actually lives
- **Domain and SSL** — your address and the padlock next to it
- **Updates** — new photos, new prices, new staff, new services
- **Fixes** — the contact form that quietly stopped sending emails in March

Most quotes cover only the first item. The rest arrives later, usually as an hourly invoice.

## The Hidden Costs Nobody Puts in the Quote

This is where budgets actually break:

- **Change requests.** A $4,000 build often comes with a $125/hour rate for anything after launch. Three small updates a month and you've added $400 to your year without noticing.
- **Going stale.** A site nobody updates slowly stops ranking, and the traffic loss doesn't show up as a bill. It shows up as a quiet phone.
- **The rebuild.** Sites built on someone's custom setup tend to need replacing in three years, because nobody else wants to touch them.

We wrote more about the traffic side of this in [5 Reasons Your Website Isn't Getting Traffic](/blog/traffic/) — most of the causes are maintenance problems, not design problems.

## Why Monthly Often Beats a Big Upfront Bill

A large upfront build treats your website as a project with an end date. It doesn't have one. The businesses that get results treat the site as something that keeps moving: new pages when they add a service, fresh content when they want to rank, automation when the admin gets heavy.

## How to Decide

Be honest about which constraint you actually have:

- **Time-rich, cash-poor?** Take the DIY builder. It genuinely works if you'll do the hours.
- **Cash-rich, need something very specific?** Hire a specialist agency for a custom build.
- **Running a business and need the site handled?** A managed plan is almost always cheaper than the true cost of the other two.

The wrong move is the middle: paying agency money for a site nobody maintains afterward. That's how a $6,000 website becomes a $6,000 brochure that's two years out of date.

## The Number That Actually Matters

Not the setup fee. Not the monthly. What matters is what the site brings in.

A site that gets you two extra customers a month pays for almost any plan on this page. A site that gets you none is expensive at any price — including free. Price the outcome, not the invoice.
